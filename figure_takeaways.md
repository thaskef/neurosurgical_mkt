# Figure Takeaways

## Drugs

**1. Revenue Bars**
Traumatic brain injury leads at $1.5B with 25% CAGR, driven by high incidence (1.4-3M US cases annually). Pituitary tumor and metastatic brain tumor follow but are declining. GBM appears twice — recurrent ($0.38B) and primary ($0.35B) — together representing $0.73B with strong growth trajectories.

**2. Incidence vs Growth**
Stroke and TBI have large patient populations but limited revenue growth, suggesting a pipeline that has not yet produced premium-priced entrants proportional to incidence. Glioma and GBM show the inverse pattern — smaller populations but stronger revenue growth, driven by targeted therapies entering at higher price points.

**3. Pipeline Intensity**
GBM and acute ischemic stroke attract the most active programs and the most companies, indicating a competitive development landscape. At the other extreme, chordoma and craniopharyngioma have few active programs but high capital raised per asset, consistent with a small number of well-funded developers committed to those rare indications.

**4. Company Matrix**
Established pharmaceutical companies (Roche, Novartis, Merck) hold the majority of marketed products across neurosurgery indications. Emerging biotech activity is concentrated in GBM, brain metastasis, and medulloblastoma — these are the indications where smaller companies are running clinical-stage programs rather than solely preclinical work.

**5. Ownership Profile**
No single company exceeds a 15% share of active assets in any major indication. Roche, Novartis, and Merck maintain the broadest presence across indications but at relatively low concentration in any single one. Smaller biotechs such as Kazia and Candel are notable for focusing their entire pipeline on one or two indications.

**6. Capital Table**
Of 553 independent companies active in neurosurgery-adjacent indications, only 89 have publicly disclosed capital raised within the past three years, totaling approximately $7B. The top ten companies account for half of that total, and capital is concentrated in GBM and neuro-oncology. The median capital raised among the 89 companies with disclosed figures is approximately $20M, suggesting most firms in this space operate with limited institutional funding or pre-revenue.

## Devices

**7. Device Pipeline**
Acute ischemic stroke leads with 62 active pipeline devices, nearly double Parkinson's disease at 29. The pipeline is organized into two primary categories: neurovascular devices (catheters, stent retrievers, thrombectomy systems) and neuromodulation devices (DBS and SCS). Across most indications, early-stage development outnumbers late-stage, indicating substantial preclinical and early development activity that has yet to reach clinical trials.

**8. Equipment Heatmap**
DBS is tightly associated with Parkinson's, depression, and epilepsy, forming a clear neuromodulation cluster. Neurovascular devices — aspiration catheters, stent retrievers, neuro microcatheters — are concentrated around stroke indications. Hydrocephalus shunts form a distinct category with minimal overlap with other device types, suggesting limited crossover between CSF management and the neurovascular or neuromodulation segments.

**9. Ownership Profile**
Medtronic and Boston Scientific hold leading positions in DBS and SCS development. Universities — including UCLA, Stanford, and Duke — are prominent among early-stage device developers, particularly in spinal cord injury and brain disease programs. Stroke device development is distributed across more than 40 companies with no single developer holding a dominant share, making it the most fragmented category in the dataset.

**10. Cumulative Launch Curve**
Cumulative projected launches for stroke devices approximately double from 2026 through 2029, the steepest trajectory among all indications. Parkinson's and epilepsy show steadier curves, consistent with the longer clinical trial and regulatory timelines typical of neuromodulation devices. Several indications exhibit multi-year periods with no expected launches, which may reflect gaps in the development pipeline or high attrition at earlier stages.

**11. Regulatory Pathway**
DBS and cortical stimulators follow the PMA pathway almost exclusively, consistent with their Class III classification and the associated regulatory burden. Catheters, stent retrievers, and shunt systems more commonly use 510(k) or CE mark pathways, enabling shorter time to market. NMPA activity appears primarily for DBS and SCS, indicating that Chinese device developers are building internal capabilities in neuromodulation rather than serving solely as contract manufacturers.

**12. Device Market Size**
Implantable neuromodulation dominates the device market at $13.0B by 2035, more than five times the next largest category. Neurovascular thrombectomy devices show the strongest growth at 7.3% CAGR, driven by expanding mechanical thrombectomy indications. Intracranial stents, though the smallest category at $127M, are growing fastest at 9.2% CAGR, reflecting an early-stage market.

**13. Neuromodulation Sub-Segments**
Spinal cord stimulators are the largest neuromodulation sub-segment at $4.8B by 2035, followed by leads ($2.8B) and sacral nerve stimulators ($2.3B). Vagus nerve stimulators show the highest growth rate at 8.3% CAGR, reflecting expanding indications beyond epilepsy into depression and inflammatory conditions. All seven sub-segments are growing at 6% or above.
